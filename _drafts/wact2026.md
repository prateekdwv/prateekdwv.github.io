At my first [WACT in Bangalore in 2019](https://www.icts.res.in/discussion-meeting/wact2019), I was just beginning my journey in algebraic complexity, unsure how to start a conversation with the pioneers of the field. Seven years later, I was sitting with Avi Wigderson in Copenhagen, listening to him talk about the people and ideas that made research a joy for him.

<!--more-->

What stayed with me was the affection with which Avi spoke about Noam Nisan. As I remember it, he talked about the pleasure of working together and the sadness he felt when Noam moved into a different area. Listening to him, I thought of myself in Bangalore, still trying to find the courage to say hello.

"We are hosting WACT this time," Nutan had told me a few months earlier, just after I returned to Copenhagen from getting married in India. When she asked if I wanted to help organise it, I said yes immediately. The workshop had given me a way into this community, and now I had a chance to help make it welcoming for others. Its [ninth edition](https://sites.google.com/view/wact2026/home) would take place in Copenhagen from 2 to 5 June 2026.

The work began quietly, with occasional meetings and invitations sent out. Then the replies started arriving. As speakers confirmed, the workshop we had been imagining became a gathering we would soon be responsible for. For Amik, Nutan, Srikanth, Amir, and me, the question was how to make those four days worth the journey.

## Shaping the Programme

As we looked through recent conference proceedings and DBLP pages, our list of results we wanted to hear about kept growing. There was far more exciting work than we could fit into four days. I had expected finding talks to be the hard part; instead, we kept having to leave out work we would have loved to hear about.

It became easier once we started thinking in terms of themes. We gave each day, and then each session, a broad direction. Longer survey talks could anchor a theme, while shorter talks presented recent work around it. That gave us a way to think about how the invitations fitted together. We hoped that grouping related talks would help participants see connections between results and carry ideas from one session into the next.

We also tried to give students opportunities to present. When a paper had a student co-author, we encouraged the student to give the talk. Nutan encouraged us to reach out more widely to women in the community and consider what support would help them attend. Her suggestions helped us broaden our search for speakers as we worked through several versions of the programme. It made me more conscious of how much the eventual speaker list depends on whom we think to approach in the first place.

The [final programme](https://sites.google.com/view/wact2026/schedule-talk-details) ranged from the permanent and determinant to proof complexity, polynomial identity testing, tensor methods, coding theory and parallel algorithms. Looking through it, I could see the balance we had been trying to find between surveys that brought us into a subject and new results that took it further.

## Four Days Are Enough

Research workshops of this kind are often five days, but Amir suggested keeping ours to four. I appreciated how often his ideas came from imagining what it would feel like to attend. Participants liked the shorter format despite the packed schedule, and I came away thinking I would choose four days again. There is only so much I can absorb after several days of talks, and a shorter workshop also asks a little less of people travelling long distances or balancing other commitments.

Those travel plans were unusually fragile in 2026. The war in the Middle East [disrupted flight routes](https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/eurocontrol-european-aviation-overview-2026-week-23), reducing capacity and making tickets both expensive and uncertain. I had never imagined geopolitics entering the planning of a workshop on such a niche topic, but it did. It made the practical side of an invitation much harder to ignore: could a young researcher afford the journey, and could they count on getting here? We spent more time than I had imagined on the arrangements that would let us all be in the same room.

## Encouraging Participation

We introduced a poster session for the first time at WACT. We wanted to give people a chance to hear a little about the work before they had to choose which posters to visit. Amik suggested asking every presenter to give a two-minute preview while everyone was still seated together.

It worked far better than I expected. Each short presentation gave the room a question, an idea or simply a poster to remember. By the time the poster session began, people already knew which conversations they wanted to continue. The reception around the posters helped too.

Amik also wanted us to preserve some physical memory of the workshop. We made a WACT 2026 poster and asked everyone to sign it. It is now framed and hanging proudly at [Basic Algorithms Research Copenhagen (BARC)](https://barc.ku.dk/). For me, those signatures bring back the people who made the four days memorable. Amik even helped me with the photographs for this post.

## A Result Arrives During the Workshop

The talk I remember most vividly was not in the original programme. The week before WACT, a visitor hinted over coffee that [Rohit Gurjar](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~rgurjar/) was part of a team that had solved a major open problem and wondered why he had not asked for a slot. We guessed that it concerned bipartite matching: Rohit had presented joint work showing that bipartite perfect matching is in quasi-NC at [WACT 2016](https://www.cs.tau.ac.il/~shpilka/wact2016/program/), and we hoped we might hear about an improvement to NC this time.

By the second day, Rohit had asked whether there might be time for a talk. We already had possible slots in mind, though it remained uncertain for a while whether the talk would happen. On Thursday, we shortened lunch to make room for a thirty-minute presentation of the [new result](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2026/100/): bipartite matching is in [NC](https://complexityzoo.net/Complexity_Zoo%3AN), joint work by Abhranil Chatterjee, Sumanta Ghosh, Rohit Gurjar, Roshan Raj and Thomas Thierauf. Rohit presented the proof and explained how the authors had arrived at it.

It was a beautiful piece of work to witness unfolding in front of us. The paper would appear later, but those of us in the room were hearing the proof and the story behind it while the discovery was still fresh. I have rarely seen a theory audience produce anything resembling a fan moment, but people were taking photographs with Rohit afterwards. We had spent months putting the programme together, yet this was a moment we could never have planned. Being able to make room for it was one of the most satisfying parts of organising WACT.

For more on the result and its significance, Nutan Limaye and Thore Husfeldt wrote a [post on the Computational Complexity blog](https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/07/bipartite-perfect-matching-in.html).

## What I Would Change

Looking back, I wish we had made more room for discussion. We put so much care into the talks, and I am glad we did, but slides and recordings would still be there after we went home. The chance to sit together and work through an idea was harder to carry away. I do not think we could leave enough of that to coffee breaks and lunch.

At a future WACT, I would prepare discussion sessions before the workshop begins. We could collect accessible open problems, ask a few participants to introduce them, and then make room for parallel groups at blackboards. I would want each group to have something to start with and someone willing to keep the conversation moving. I would be reluctant to set aside an hour called "discussion time" and simply hope it worked.

I am also less convinced now that a long lunch break is always helpful. I understand the hope that people will use it for informal conversations, but after two or three days of talks, I find a long interruption can make it harder to settle back into the afternoon. I would prefer a shorter lunch, an earlier end to the formal programme, and a longer stretch afterwards when people can talk without watching the clock or saving attention for the next lecture.

I kept thinking, too, about what the workshop felt like for someone attending for the first time. I remembered how easily a room full of potential collaborators could look like a room full of people who already knew one another. We organised senior--junior lunches to lower that barrier. Nutan held a separate lunch for women participants, and the poster reception gave students another natural way to take part. These initiatives helped, though I think we can do more. I would like someone arriving as unsure as I was in Bangalore to find it a little easier to join a conversation.

I am grateful, too, to the wonderful volunteers whose support we depended on throughout the workshop.

A few weeks later, the organising team went out for dinner. Someone asked whether I would do it again, knowing how much time it demanded. I said yes immediately.

Organising WACT took time away from research, but I do not consider that time lost. It showed me that I could contribute to this community without a famous name or a large group of my own. I could help make room for a student to present their work, for an unfinished idea to find a collaborator, or for an unexpected result to be shared while it was still fresh. WACT had given me a place to begin in 2019. Helping make that possible for someone else felt worth the time.
