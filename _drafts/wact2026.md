I had just returned to Copenhagen after getting married in India when Nutan told me, "We are hosting WACT this time." She asked if I wanted to join the organising team. I was on board immediately.

The Workshop on Algebraic Complexity Theory began in Aarhus in 2013. Its [ninth edition](https://sites.google.com/view/wact2026/home) was held in Copenhagen from 2 to 5 June 2026. My own research journey began with [WACT 2019 at ICTS Bangalore](https://www.icts.res.in/discussion-meeting/wact2019), so the workshop has a special place in my heart. Getting to help organise it seven years later meant a lot to me.

The first few months were quiet. An occasional meeting, a long list of people to contact, and invitations sent across the community. Then speakers started confirming. When Avi Wigderson accepted our invitation, followed by several other wonderful researchers, the workshop began to feel real. Our organising team---Amik, Nutan, Srikanth, Amir, and me---now had to turn an impressive list of possibilities into a coherent programme.

<!--more-->

## Choosing What Not to Include

For this WACT, we decided to curate the talks by going through recent conference proceedings and DBLP pages, looking for results that the algebraic complexity community would enjoy hearing about. Finding good papers was easy enough, but with only so many slots in the programme, the harder task was deciding which ones to leave out.

Themes made the process manageable. We gave each day, and then each session, a broad direction. Longer survey talks could anchor a theme, while shorter talks presented recent work around it. This was more useful than treating every invitation as an independent choice. A workshop programme should amount to more than a collection of individually good talks. The talks should speak to one another.

We also tried to give students opportunities to present. When a paper had a student co-author, we encouraged the student to give the talk. Nutan encouraged us to reach out more widely to women in the community and consider what support would help them attend. Her suggestions helped us broaden our search for speakers as we worked through several versions of the programme. It made me more conscious of how much the eventual speaker list depends on whom we think to approach in the first place.

The [final programme](https://sites.google.com/view/wact2026/schedule-talk-details) ranged from the permanent and determinant to proof complexity, polynomial identity testing, tensor methods, coding theory and parallel algorithms. It also mixed surveys with new results. That range was deliberate.

## Four Days Are Enough

Research workshops of this kind are often five days, but Amir suggested keeping ours to four. Participants appreciated the shorter format despite the packed schedule, and I now think four days should be the default. After several days of talks, an extra day does not necessarily mean more understanding, while a shorter workshop makes attendance easier for people travelling long distances or balancing other commitments.

Those travel plans were unusually fragile in 2026. The war in the Middle East [disrupted flight routes](https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/eurocontrol-european-aviation-overview-2026-week-23), reducing capacity and making tickets both expensive and uncertain. I had never imagined geopolitics entering the planning of a workshop on such a niche topic, but it did. An invitation means little if a young researcher cannot afford the journey, or cannot be confident that the journey will happen. We spent considerable time dealing with arrangements that had nothing to do with algebraic complexity and everything to do with whether the community could be in the same room.

## Encouraging Participation

We introduced a poster session for the first time at WACT. In a busy session, participants may not know which posters they would like to visit, and interesting work can be easy to miss. Amik suggested asking every presenter to give a two-minute preview while everyone was still seated together.

It worked far better than I expected. Each short presentation gave the room a question, an idea or simply a poster to remember. By the time the poster session began, people already knew which conversations they wanted to continue. The reception around the posters helped too.

Amik also wanted us to preserve some physical memory of the workshop. We made a WACT 2026 poster and asked everyone to sign it. It is now framed and hanging proudly at [Basic Algorithms Research Copenhagen (BARC)](https://barc.ku.dk/). Academic events leave behind papers, slides and photographs, but the signed poster feels like a more personal record of the people who made those four days memorable.

## A Result Arrives During the Workshop

The most memorable talk was not in the original programme. The week before WACT, a visitor hinted over coffee that [Rohit Gurjar](https://www.cse.iitb.ac.in/~rgurjar/) was part of a team that had solved a major open problem and wondered why he had not asked for a slot. We guessed that it concerned bipartite matching: Rohit had presented joint work showing that bipartite perfect matching is in quasi-NC at [WACT 2016](https://www.cs.tau.ac.il/~shpilka/wact2016/program/), and we hoped we might hear about an improvement to NC this time.

By the second day, Rohit had asked whether there might be time for a talk. We already had possible slots in mind, though it remained uncertain for a while whether the talk would happen. On Thursday, we shortened lunch to make room for a thirty-minute presentation of the [new result](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2026/100/): bipartite matching is in [NC](https://complexityzoo.net/Complexity_Zoo%3AN), joint work by Abhranil Chatterjee, Sumanta Ghosh, Rohit Gurjar, Roshan Raj and Thomas Thierauf. Rohit presented the proof and explained how the authors had arrived at it.

It was a beautiful piece of work to witness unfolding in front of us. The paper would appear later, but those of us in the room were hearing the proof and the story behind it while the discovery was still fresh. I have rarely seen a theory audience produce anything resembling a fan moment, but people were taking photographs with Rohit afterwards. We had spent months putting the programme together, yet this was a moment we could never have planned. Being able to make room for it was one of the most satisfying parts of organising WACT.

For more on the result and its significance, Nutan Limaye and Thore Husfeldt wrote a [post on the Computational Complexity blog](https://blog.computationalcomplexity.org/2026/07/bipartite-perfect-matching-in.html).

## What I Would Change

Talks are the centre of a research workshop, but they should not consume all of it. Slides and recordings can carry information long after everyone goes home. What requires people to be together is discussion, and I do not think coffee breaks and lunch are enough to make good discussions happen.

At a future WACT, I would prepare discussion sessions before the workshop begins. We could collect accessible open problems, ask a few participants to introduce them, and then make room for parallel groups at blackboards. A useful session needs a starting point and someone willing to keep it moving. Simply placing researchers in a room for an hour and calling it "discussion time" is unlikely to work.

I am also unconvinced by the convention of long lunch breaks. The usual argument is that lunch gives people time for informal conversations. In practice, after two or three days of talks, a long interruption often makes the afternoon harder. I would prefer a shorter lunch, an earlier end to the formal programme, and a longer stretch afterwards when people can talk without watching the clock or saving attention for the next lecture.

Young participants need particular care. For a senior researcher, a crowded workshop is a room full of collaborators and old friends. For a student, the same room can be a list of people who already seem to know one another. We organised senior--junior lunches to lower that barrier. Nutan held a separate lunch for women participants, and the poster reception gave students another natural way to take part. These initiatives helped, though I think we can do more. A workshop becomes a community event only when its newest members can enter its conversations.

A few weeks later, the organising team went out for dinner. Someone asked whether I would do it again, knowing how much time it demanded. I said yes immediately.

Organising WACT took time away from research, but I do not consider that time lost. Researchers like me, without a famous name or a large group of our own, can still help build a useful platform for a community. Research needs good papers. It also needs places where a student gets their first serious audience, where an unfinished idea finds the right collaborator, and where an unexpected result can be shared before it has even reached the internet. Helping create such a place felt as important to me as doing the research itself.
